## Planning Your Deck: Design, Materials and Budget Basics

The decisions you make before construction starts have the biggest effect on how the deck feels and what it costs. Here is how we think about them with every homeowner.

### Start with how you will use the deck

Before choosing materials, think about how the space will really work. Will you eat out there, or mostly relax? Do you need room for a grill, a table and chairs, or a hot tub? Do you want shade, privacy or a view? Is the deck close to the kitchen door, or does it need to connect to the yard? The answers decide the size, the shape and where the stairs go.

### Design choices

- **A ground level deck** sits low to the yard. It is usually the simplest and most budget friendly option, and it often needs less structure and fewer railings.
- **A raised deck** comes off the back of the house at the height of the main floor. It gives you easy access from inside and often a better view, and it needs stronger framing, stairs and railings.
- **A multi level deck** steps down a slope or breaks a large space into zones for dining, lounging and cooking. It adds design interest and also adds structure and cost.

### Decking materials and how long they typically last

Every deck differs, and lifespan depends on construction, drainage and upkeep. As general industry guidance, typical ranges look like this:

| Material | Typical lifespan |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure treated wood | About 10 to 20 years |
| Cedar | About 15 to 25 years |
| Composite decking | About 25 to 30 years or more |
| PVC decking | About 25 to 30 years or more |

These are planning guides, not a promise about your deck. Wood costs less up front and needs regular cleaning and sealing. Composite and PVC cost more up front and need much less upkeep. Your real numbers depend on your home, your choices and the scope of work, and we put them in writing after the consultation.

### Keep a contingency

We recommend keeping 10% to 15% of the budget in reserve. When an old deck comes off, we sometimes find rot in the house framing, damaged siding or a ledger that was never flashed correctly. A reserve turns a surprise into a manageable decision instead of a crisis.

### Permits

Many decks need a permit, especially decks attached to the house, raised decks and decks above a certain height. Simple ground level decks may not. Rules differ between Seattle, Bellevue and other cities, so we check what applies to your project during the planning stage.

## Built for Pacific Northwest Rain: Where Quality Actually Matters

A deck lives outdoors in one of the wettest climates in the country. The parts that decide how long it lasts are mostly the ones you cannot see once it is built. Here is how we think about them.

### Footings and structure

A deck is only as strong as what is under it. Footings need to be the right size and depth for the soil and the load, and the framing needs the right spacing, size and connections. A deck that moves, bounces or leans has a structure problem, and decking boards on top will not fix it.

### The connection to your house

Where a deck attaches to the house is the most important detail on the project. The ledger needs to be fastened properly and flashed so water runs away from the house instead of into it. When this detail fails, water gets behind the siding and into the framing, and the damage can be serious and expensive. We take this detail seriously on every attached deck.

### Drainage and airflow

Wood stays healthy when it can dry out. Proper gaps between boards, a slight pitch away from the house and airflow under the deck keep moisture from sitting in the framing. A deck that is boxed in with no ventilation tends to rot earlier.

### Railings and stairs

Railings and stairs are about safety. Height, post connections, baluster spacing and stair rise and run all matter, and building codes cover them for good reason. A railing that feels solid when you lean on it is one that was built and connected well.

### Where we suggest spending, and where to save

Spend on the things you cannot easily redo: footings, framing, flashing, hardware and good installation. Save on things that are easy to change later, such as furniture, lighting and planters. If the budget is tight, a smaller deck built well is better than a larger deck built cheaply. Our job is to help you put the budget where it protects your investment.

## Living Through a Deck Project

A deck project is smaller than a remodel, but it still disrupts your yard for a while. Here is what to expect and how we make it easier.

- **Timeline.** Planning and design usually take a few weeks, and permits can add time. Construction itself typically takes from a few days for a simple deck to a few weeks for a larger or multi level one. Weather in the Puget Sound area can move the schedule, and we tell you plainly if it does. We give you a specific schedule in writing once the plan is set.
- **Access and storage.** We need room to bring in materials and store them, and to get equipment to the work area. Move vehicles, furniture and planters out of the way, and let us know about gates, sprinklers and anything buried in the yard.
- **Noise and dust.** Demolition and framing are noisy, and cutting creates sawdust. We keep the work area contained and clean up at the end of every work day.
- **Pets and children.** Keep them away from the work zone. We will tell you when the noisiest days are so you can plan around them.
- **Decisions.** We will ask you to choose materials, colors and railing styles on a schedule, because late changes slow the project and add cost. We help you make each choice in time.
- **Communication.** You will know who is on site, what is happening this week and what comes next. If something unexpected appears when we open up the old deck, we explain it, show you the options and agree on the next step with you before we continue.

A well planned deck project feels organized even on the messiest day, and that is what we aim for.

## Deck Building FAQs

### How long does it take to build a deck?

It depends on the size, the height and the design. A simple ground level deck takes much less time than a large raised or multi level deck. Most of the schedule is made up of design, permits where needed and material lead times, with construction after that. After your consultation we give you a written schedule for your specific project.

### How much does a deck cost?

The cost depends on the size, the height, the materials, the railings and stairs, and how complex the site is. The fastest way to get a real number is a free consultation, where we look at your yard and give you a clear written estimate.

### Do I need a permit to build a deck?

Often yes, especially for decks attached to the house or raised above a certain height. Simple ground level decks may not need one. Requirements differ between Seattle, Bellevue and other cities, and we help you with the permit requirements for your project.

### Should I choose wood or composite?

It depends on your budget and how much upkeep you want. Wood costs less up front and needs regular cleaning and sealing. Composite costs more up front and needs much less maintenance. We will go through both with you and tell you honestly which fits how you will use the deck.

### Can you replace my old deck?

Yes. Removing an old or unsafe deck and building a new one is a common project. We inspect the structure and the connection to your house first, and tell you whether a full replacement or a repair makes more sense.

### How long will my deck last?

It depends on the materials, the construction and the upkeep. A well built deck with good footings, proper flashing and good drainage lasts much longer than one built without those details. Wood decks last longer when they are cleaned and sealed on a regular schedule.

### Can a deck be built in the rain or in winter?

Many parts of the work can go on in cool weather, but heavy rain slows things down, and some finishes need dry conditions. We plan around the forecast and tell you plainly if weather changes the schedule.

### What happens at the free consultation?

We visit your home, look at your yard, and talk about your goals, your wish list and your budget. You do not need a finished design. Bring photos or ideas if you have them. You will leave with a clear idea of the options, and we follow up with a written estimate.
